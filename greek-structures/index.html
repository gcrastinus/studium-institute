II

Overview

The Greek world was a network of some 1,000–1,500 small, self-governing city-states (1,035 are documented). Each rested on small farms and sea trade. Each had simple written laws that its citizens could change, and a religion with no priestly caste and no god-given law code.

The argument of this course concerns form. The most splendid building in the city was the temple, wrapped in columns and facing outward, while the house turned inward around a courtyard. The city’s main institutions (assembly, law court, phalanx, games and theatre) share one pattern: a gathered public, and speakers or performers drawn from among them. Public speech, in oratory and in sung poetry, runs through all of them.

From this setting come the major genres: epic recitation at feasts, lyric at the symposium, choral odes for victors, and Athenian drama. The period from the Persian Wars to Alexander (490–330 BC) is the brief, decisive “moment” of the Classical city, before the Greek world passes into empire.

The polis

Small scale, citizen farmers, colonies across the Mediterranean and the Black Sea.

Built forms

Temple (outward) vs. house (inward); council house, prytaneion, agora, stoa.

Public + speakers

Assembly, courts, phalanx, sport, theatre, with discourse and inquiry at their heart.

Arts in their setting

Epic, lyric, choral song and drama, each tied to a place and an occasion.

The long view

1000–500 BC growth · 490–330 BC the Classical moment · 330 BC – AD 1453 empire.
