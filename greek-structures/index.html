II

Overview

The handout describes the Greek world as a network of some 1,500 small, self-governing city-states. Each rested on small farms and sea trade. Each had simple written laws that its citizens could change, and a religion without a priestly caste or god-given law.

Its argument concerns form. The most splendid building in the city was the temple, wrapped in columns and facing outward, while the house turned inward around a courtyard. The city’s main institutions (assembly, law court, phalanx, games and theatre) share one pattern: a gathered public, and speakers or performers drawn from among them. Public speech, in oratory and in sung poetry, runs through all of them.

From this setting come the major genres: epic recitation at feasts, lyric at the symposium, choral odes for victors, and Athenian drama. The period from the Persian Wars to Alexander (490–330) is the brief, decisive “moment” of the Classical city, before the Greek world passes into empire.

The polis

Small scale, citizen farmers, colonies across the Mediterranean.

Built forms

Temple (outward) vs. house (inward); council house, prytaneion, agora.

Public + speakers

Assembly, courts, phalanx, sport, theatre, with discourse and inquiry at their heart.

Arts in their setting

Epic, lyric, choral song and drama, each tied to a place and an occasion.

The long view

1000–500 growth · 490–330 the Classical moment · 330–1453 empire.
