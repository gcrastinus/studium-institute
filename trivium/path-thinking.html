Critical Thinking as a Starting Point

The desire to skip ahead to “critical thinking” is often a desire for what logic really is: seeing what follows logically from what, spotting a bad inference, and being clear about one's potential biases. This is obviously a good thing. Someone who cannot tell a contradiction from a mere disagreement will likely have many problems in ordinary life just as in the sciences.

Grammar, as it is often taught nowadays, can seem to be a list of corrections and rules and arbitrary preferences. If that is what “grammar” means, the thought that it is not something that needs to be put first when we turn to higher studies makes sense. The same is true of rhetoric, at least in some parts. Many things in rhetoric do, of course, seem relevant to critical thinking, like knowing how others are trying to persuade us, but much of it may seem unnecessary. Why should we consider in detail the parts of a speech and things like rhetorical figures? It can seem better to go straight to logic, and in particular practical logic looking at real arguments and puzzles.

It can certainly be profitable to examine detailed arguments about real subject matters, especially if students are not going to take a more formal course in logic; this is often the best substitute. But an informal course cannot resolve the deeper questions and at least of the examples will require an answer about how reasoning really works at the most fundamental level. If that deeper level never gets explained, then students are left with just a course that teaches yet more rules and examples, rather that a course that shows them why reasoning must be one way rather than another.

Puzzles and brain-teasers can give us a first taste of reasoning and necessity: if these premises are granted, that conclusion follows from them necessarily. A grid in a logic game, where we have to meet many different conditions to work out who went where and when, requires real reasoning and real inference, even if it is not studied systematically. An interest in logic games and puzzles is a first step toward being drawn into the art of reasoning itself; puzzles like these can be one of the ways a student first feels that thinking has a discipline, something determinate and fixed.

What is right, then, is the desire to reason well and avoid mistakes; but there is a great deal more to reasoning than this, both in what we can be clear about and in what strategies we can learn to help us become our best. The art of reasoning well depends, moreover, on first being clear about our words: how they come to mean what they mean, and how to use them correctly in sentences and paragraphs. And the art of reasoning itself points toward a further art, the art of persuasion.
