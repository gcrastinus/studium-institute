What kind of sign a word is

❧

The order already set out says what a word reaches; it does not yet say what a word is. For that we need one more distinction, and it is the one that answers the oldest objection to this whole course, namely, that words are labels stuck on things and that the things are what matter.

Two cuts across signs

Houser makes the division with two cuts. The first is familiar: a sign may be natural, like smoke for fire or a rash for a fever, or conventional, holding its meaning because we assigned it or because use has fixed it, like a red octagon, a handshake, or the word dog.

The second cut is the decisive one: a sign may be instrumental or formal. This cut concerns knowing, and in particular the sign-vehicle, the thing that carries the sign relation, such as the smoke or the sound. Both kinds of sign are relations.

An instrumental sign must first be known as an object before it leads to another. Smoke is a real grey plume; we know the plume, and then, through it, the fire. A stop sign is a painted piece of metal, and we have to see the metal before we can take the order.

A formal sign, by contrast, leads to its object without first being known as an object itself. As Houser puts it, “their fundamental reality is that they are signs of something else.” Our awareness, moreover, is ordered the other way: we notice the object first, and only later, by reflection, notice the sign at all. His example is a bear seeing a salmon, which should be taken as Houser’s analogy: the bear notices the salmon and never its own seeing. External seeing, on this account, does not form a concept of its own; the formal sign properly speaking is the concept. A concept is a likeness of its object by nature, and the same for all. It has being of its own, as a quality of the intellect; what it does not do is present itself as an object before it presents what it is of.

If we cross the two cuts, there are four possibilities, of which three exist: natural and instrumental, like smoke; conventional and instrumental, like the stop sign; and natural and formal, like our concepts. There are no conventional formal signs, because imposition (the assigning of a sign to signify something) needs a vehicle already known as an object, and a formal sign is never already known as an object.

The word and the concept can now be placed in this division.

Natural or conventional?Instrumental or formal?
A spoken or written wordConventional. We assign it, or use fixes it.Instrumental. It is known as an object first.
A conceptNatural. It is a likeness of its object by nature, and the same for all.Formal. It leads to its object without first being known as an object.

Houser states it in a single line: a written or spoken term is a conventional and instrumental sign; a mental term is a natural and formal sign. Maritain classes the word the same way in passing, when he distinguishes a concept of a thing from a concept of a word: the written or spoken word is “the instrumental sign … which, being known the first, leads to the knowledge of the thing itself.” It is being known first that makes it instrumental.

Why the account of words as labels fails

Now consider the objection. A label, in the thin sense, is something that can be removed, and the thing is unchanged. The objection says that a name is like that, and that serious people should attend to the thing instead.

Half of that is right, and it is the half the tradition asserts first. A word really is a conventional instrumental sign: it is known as an object first, we assigned it or use has fixed it, and it can be replaced. We can replace glacier with another sound; Latin does.

The objection fails at the next step. It assumes that because the word is a removable label, naming as a whole is nothing but a removable label, so that between us and the glacier there is nothing but a removable noise. But between us and the glacier there is also a concept, and a concept is not that kind of sign. It cannot be removed in that way, because it was never attached as a label. A concept is a likeness of its object by nature, and the same for all; it leads us to the glacier without first being known as an object itself. It is, in fact, the glacier as understood by us. So, “Never mind the words, look at the thing” tells us to attend to the thing while setting aside the concept by which the thing is understood.

So the honest form of the objection turns into a demand with which this course agrees: attend to the thing. But the way to attend to a thing is to get the concept right, and the only instrument we have for working on a concept (keeping it fixed, testing it, and communicating it to someone else) is the word. Walshe puts the matter in one image:

A parrot can make vocal sounds identical to words used in human language, yet they are not words since they do not signify things through thoughts.
Walshe, Commentary on the Categories of Aristotle, “What is Logic About?”

The parrot has the sound and nothing that signifies through a thought, and the same is true of a fluent page that has no speaker behind it.

This is why the logician’s subject is stated as it is. Walshe says that the proximate matter of logic is words insofar as they signify things through thoughts, and that the remote matter is the thoughts themselves. Andres says it more briefly, and it is worth memorizing in his form: logic is about words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts. That names what logic works on; what it studies goes further. Logic studies the relations things have only because they are known: genus, species, subject, predicate, statement, and conclusion. These are called second intentions. The logician cannot get at a concept except through a word, and neither can we.

Where truth enters

One more thing follows, and it is the reason there is a second art at all. Aristotle continues a few lines later:

As there are in the mind thoughts which do not involve truth or falsity, and also those which must be either true or false, so it is in speech. For truth and falsity imply combination and separation. Nouns and verbs, provided nothing is added, are like thoughts without combination or separation; “man” and “white,” as isolated terms, are not yet either true or false.
Aristotle, On Interpretation 1, 16a9–18, Edghill translation, as printed in Andres.

Consider the word white said aloud: we have signified something, but we have said nothing true and nothing false. If we say the shirt is white, we have. Truth and falsity arrive with combination and separation, not with naming.

A few lines further on, Aristotle draws a consequence that bears directly on the first page of this course, namely, that not every sentence is a statement:

Yet not every sentence is a statement; only such are statements as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false.
Aristotle, On Interpretation 4, 16b26–17a4, Edghill translation, as printed in Andres.

The foundation opened with prayer as an act of speech, and it is one; but it is speech that is not a statement. Aristotle sets it aside here, not because it is unimportant, but because it is not what the logic of truth is about, and in the same sentence he assigns it to rhetoric and poetry. The third art will take it up.

That single observation divides the first two arts. Grammar composes names into a saying, and it can succeed or fail at composing; but a well-composed saying is not thereby a true one. Truth is not in the term; it is in what the mind does when it joins a predicate to a subject and affirms or denies. That is the second act of the mind, judgment, and it is what logic takes up.
