Words as signs

❧

We have seen that a word is a conventional sign of a concept, and that a concept is a formal sign of a thing. We can now consider words more closely: what they do, how they relate to one another, and what part of them belongs to logic.

What logic takes from words

The study of logic is concerned with words, but only in so far as reasoning is carried out by means of them. Whether a word is used correctly in a given language is a question for grammar. Logic asks what the word signifies, through our concepts, and how what it signifies is related to what other words signify. Logic, then, studies words in so far as they signify things through our thoughts.

How words relate to one another

Words can stand in several relations to one another. Three are especially important at the start.

  • Synonyms are different words for the same concept, such as car and automobile.
  • Related words share a meaning but differ in form and in what they name, such as courage and courageous. One names a virtue; the other names someone who has it.
  • Equivocal words are one word for two or more concepts, such as bat, which can mean an animal or a piece of sporting equipment.

Many disagreements turn out to be equivocations. Two people use the same word, but each means something different by it, and neither notices. Since the sound is the conventional part of the sign, the sound alone will not show the difference; the difference is in the concept. So the check has to be made by asking what is meant, and the usual answer is a definition. A definition states the one concept that a word is carrying, so that we can use the word in the same sense more than once.

Simple and complex sayings

Some sayings are simple: man, white, runs. Others are complex: the man is white, the dog runs. A simple saying signifies something, but it is neither true nor false. If we say white aloud, we have signified a color, but we have said nothing true or false about anything. If we say the shirt is white, we have said something that is either true or false.

So, truth and falsity do not belong to single words; they belong to statements, in which something is affirmed or denied of something else. Aristotle notes that not every complex saying is a statement in this sense: a prayer, a question, or a command is a sentence, but it is neither true nor false (On Interpretation 4). These sentences belong to rhetoric and poetry rather than to the logic of truth.

Words are conventional, concepts are not

It is often said that words are only labels and that what matters is the things. That is partly true. The sound of a word is conventional; we can replace it, and other languages do. But the concept that the word signifies is not conventional; it is a likeness of the thing and is the same for all. So, to attend to the things, we must get our concepts right; and the only instrument we have for fixing, testing, and sharing our concepts is the word. For that reason the study of words is not a distraction from things but the way to them.
