Words as Signs

What a sign is

John of St. Thomas gives the classical definition:

A sign is that which makes present something other than itself to a cognitive power.
John of St. Thomas, Summulae I, 2.

A sign, then, has three aspects: it makes something present to something else, what it makes present is something other than itself, and it makes that thing present to a knowing power, such as sight or the intellect. Smoke on a hill signifies fire to someone who knows that smoke comes from fire but it does not signify anything to a stone, since a stone has no cognitive power of any kind. (Sometimes, signs are said to "represent" something to us; but this does not mean they necessarily have a resemblance to the thing in question; it means something different; it means that they re-present or make present again the thing in question to the knowing power.)

Signs are first divided into formal and instrumental. Instrumental signs are natural or conventional.

  1. Formal signs make things present to us through themselves, through what they are in themselves. These are what our concepts are: formal signs. Note that when we understand a tree, we do not first notice our concept and then infer the tree; we know the tree through the concept, and we notice the concept only later, by reflecting on what we know.
  2. Instrumental signs must first be known themselves before they lead us to something else. They are of two kinds:
    1. Natural signs are caused by what they signify, and we know the sign first and then what it signifies. Smoke is a natural sign of fire, and tracks in the snow are a natural sign of the animal that made them.
    2. Conventional signs signify by agreement or by custom, so they can be given almost any meaning. A road arrow and the sounds of a spoken language are conventional signs.

So, a spoken or written word is a conventional, and therefore instrumental, sign, and a concept is a formal sign.

Our words, therefore, signify what we understand, and what we understand is the thing that we are speaking about itself, known through the concept. For this reason, the concept is that by which we know the thing, not something that stands in the way. Our words, however, to be clear, are not only names for things; words play many other roles in expression, some of which only have significance by relation to other words, for example, “and” or “if.”

This order also shows something about the purposes of language. Socially, the first purpose of language is communication in human social groups and for common purposes. But language is very often the primary way that we make the world present to ourselves and only then the way we communicate what we have learned to others. Ultimately and at root, the mind understands things through its concepts but the mind uses language to give stability to its thoughts and make them easy to recall and the mind uses language further to give order to its various acts. Language is, therefore, the chief instrument that aids the mind in carrying out its effort to set our thoughts in order.

Philosophical Exploration: Is language an instrument, or the medium we live in?

Gadamer and Heidegger both object to calling language an instrument. For Heidegger, language is "the house of being," a place within what makes up the world. For Gadamer, language is the medium in which we live and understand, and not a tool that we pick up after we already have our thoughts. But these views and objections are not always very far from the classical tradition. In Truth and Method, Part III, Gadamer praises the Augustinian and Thomist teaching on the inner word as an alternative to the instrumental theory of signs that came to dominate modern thought.

A good deal of what Heidegger and Gadamer insist on is true, and the classical account can incorporate it or already did in earlier formulations. Language is not like a hammer that we can put down. We think most often by inner expression in a language, we learn much of what we know through language and within it, and the words we inherit from our cultures shape what we are able to notice. Language therefore cannot be only a set of conventional labels that we first know and then use; and contemporary philosophers are right to reject such a claim.

It helps to say what "instrument" means in the classical tradition: that by which the mind acts. An instrument in this sense is not outside the one who uses it. Aristotle calls the hand "the instrument of instruments" (De Anima III.8), and, clearly, the hand belongs to the person who acts with it. Language is the chief instrument of the mind in the same way. It gives stability and order to our thoughts, and this is compatible with language as the medium in which the mind carries out its thinking. Language is, nevertheless, especially close to us: we live in a kind of stream of thought and language that informs and is informed by everything we concern ourselves with. In understanding the world and each other, we mostly attend to what is said and not to the words themselves, and this is just what the classical account says about concepts, that they are formal signs.

The inner word is where the tradition answers Gadamer most directly. For Augustine and Aquinas, the inner word (verbum mentis) is the concept that the mind forms in understanding; this inner word is like a word in that the mind asserts it to itself as the term of its act of understanding; and it is the spoken word that expresses it. So thought does not first exist without language and then acquire outer tools. Language is rooted in the mind's own act of understanding. The tradition adds that the inner word is a likeness of the thing, so that it keeps us open to the thing, while outer words are conventional, which is why different languages can say the same thing in different words.

Our course of study here does not take up the inner word because it belongs to the study of the soul.

Sources: Hans-Georg Gadamer, Truth and Method, Part III. Martin Heidegger, "Letter on Humanism" (1947) and On the Way to Language (1959). Augustine, De Trinitate XV. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 34, a. 1, and De Veritate q. 4, a. 1. Aristotle, De Anima III.8.

How the mind forms the concept within itself (the “inner word”) is a question about the soul rather than about logic. That is considered in philosophical anthropology and is not discussed here.
