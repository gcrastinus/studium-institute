The third art

Rhetoric ordered to the true and the good

❧

We have all seen a true thing said and not received. A proof was on the table, but the hearer was tired, or hostile, or lost by the second sentence, and nothing followed in the room. We have also seen the reverse: a speech that moved people, after which we could not say what had been shown. Rhetoric begins in those two failures, since grammar can succeed, and logic can succeed, and the hearer may still not see it.

Much human discourse also involves persuasion, since even if we express the truth to someone, he may not be persuaded, and then we have not shown him the relevant truth. This assumes that what we are expressing is true, but, of course, sometimes it is not. What is it that persuades someone to accept something as true? How can we best find the means to persuade others of something true? And how can we safeguard truth from the art of persuasion, which anyone skilled in it can use to convince us of something false?

This lesson takes those questions as the reason the third art exists, and as the reason it is not a license to win.

Aristotle opens the Rhetoric by pairing the art with dialectic, not with a science of one subject; both deal with things that can go either way. Then he gives the working definition:

Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion.
Aristotle, Rhetoric I.2, trans. W. Rhys Roberts.

Rhetoric, then, is not the art of winning. Its first work is to see what can be said, in this case, for these hearers. The function of the art, he adds, is not simply to succeed in persuading, but to discover the means of coming as near such success as the circumstances allow, just as medicine’s function is not simply to make every patient quite healthy. A speech may be well made and still fail with a particular crowd; that does not mean the art was something else.

Rhetoric is the counterpart of Dialectic.
Aristotle, Rhetoric I.1, trans. W. Rhys Roberts.

Counterpart, however, does not mean replacement. Rhetoric uses probable speech suited to an audience: the enthymeme, which is a kind of syllogism for matters that can be otherwise, often with a premise the hearers already supply; and the example, which is a kind of induction. Demonstration, when it can be had, remains demonstration. The speaker who has only a proof, and no care for these hearers in this case, has stopped at logic and called the job done. The speaker who has only heat, or only figures, has not yet practiced this art; he has practiced a rival of it.

The art is ordered to the true and the good, not to sophistry. Aristotle is explicit that we study how persuasion can be used on opposite sides, not in order that we may in practice employ it both ways (“for we must not make people believe what is wrong”), but in order to see the facts and to confute unfair argument. What makes a man a sophist is not his faculty but his moral purpose. Things that are true and things that are better are, by their nature, practically always easier to prove and easier to believe in. That is the hope this course keeps. It is also why the art must be learned: before some audiences, even the exactest knowledge will not by itself produce conviction, because argument based on knowledge implies instruction, and there are hearers one cannot instruct as one instructs a geometer.

Of the means of persuasion that the speech itself furnishes, Aristotle names three. Ethos lies in the speaker, as the speech shows him to be trustworthy, and not in a reputation imported from outside the oration. Pathos lies in putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind, because we do not judge in the same way when pleased and when pained. Logos lies in the speech itself, when we have proved a truth or an apparent truth by suitable arguments. Houser, who treats rhetoric before logic in his textbook, likewise begins from three appeals. This course uses Aristotle’s names. The practice application will drill them on real speeches; this page will not.

The hearer’s office also divides the art. A speech that asks an assembly what to do is not the same as one that asks a jury what was done, or one that asks a gathering to praise or blame. Deliberative, forensic, and epideictic: there are three species because there are three offices of the hearer. (Roberts’s English for the first and third is “political” oratory and “ceremonial oratory of display.”) Arrangement and style come after invention, because figures without a case are a box of ornaments. The application follows that order, keeping Augustine’s three offices (to teach, to delight, to move) as offices of showing, not as a substitute for showing.

A sign is speculative when its object is to be understood, and practical when its object is to be done. A command signifies practically, and deliberative speech, which asks an assembly what to do, works in that practical order. A speech that only sets a truth before the mind signifies speculatively. This is why Aristotle sends prayer, command, and wish out of the logic of truth and toward rhetoric and poetry.

If empty debate (fluency without a claim that can be meant) is already your difficulty, you may have been sent here sooner than the default order. Do this work, and then return to grammar and to logic. Rhetoric cannot show a hearer what has not been said, and it cannot be ordered to the true if one cannot yet tell what follows logically. The default order remains grammar, then logic, then this art.

This lesson uses those questions and Aristotle’s definitions. It does not invent a later account of persuasion beyond them.
