The third art

Rhetoric

Even when what we say is correct and true, an audience may not be persuaded. And the reverse also happens: a speech can move people without showing them anything. Rhetoric is the art that concerns persuasion toward truth, and it raises three questions. What persuades someone to accept something as true? How can we best find the means to persuade others of what is true? And how can we guard against those who use the same art to persuade us of what is false?

What rhetoric is

Aristotle calls rhetoric the counterpart of dialectic, since both concern matters that can be otherwise and reason from accepted opinions. He defines it as “the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion” (Rhetoric I.2). So, the first task of rhetoric is not to win but to see what can be said in this case and to these hearers. A speech or article may be well made and still fail to persuade, just as medicine may be well practiced and still not cure the person.

Rhetoric reasons in its own forms. The enthymeme is a syllogism about matters that can be one way or another, often with a premise left for the hearers to supply; the example is a kind of induction. Demonstration, where it can be had, remains demonstration; rhetoric does not replace it, but rhetoric does use it when it can.

The means of persuasion

Aristotle names three means of persuasion that the speech itself provides.

  • Ethos: the character of the speaker, as the speech shows him to be trustworthy.
  • Pathos: the disposition of the hearer, since we do not judge in the same way when we are pleased and when we are pained.
  • Logos: the argument of the speech itself, when it shows something true or apparently true.

The kinds of speech

There are three kinds of speech, according to what the hearer is asked to judge. Deliberative speech asks an assembly what to do; forensic speech asks a court what was done; and epideictic speech asks a gathering to praise or blame. After invention, that is, finding what can be said, come arrangement and style.

Rhetoric and truth

Rhetoric is ordered to the true and the good. Aristotle says that we study how to argue both sides of a question not in order to persuade people of what is wrong but in order to see the facts clearly and to refute unfair arguments; what makes someone a sophist (i.e., a manipulator who uses rhetoric in order to deceive) is not his skill but his purpose. Aristotle and the tradition teach also that what is true and good is by nature easier to prove and to believe. But some hearers cannot be instructed the way a student of geometry is instructed, and so the art is needed even by those who know the truth.
