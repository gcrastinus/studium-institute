Why training our speech is earlier than finding a voice

❧

Rhetoric, in this course, is the art of finding and ordering the available means of persuasion in speech, ordered to what is true and good and not merely to persuading. In a certain sense, of course, rhetoric and its use do not wait until we have mastered the art of grammar; this is true simply because we speak, read, and persuade others all the time without having mastered grammar.

Nevertheless, rhetoric does presuppose grammar and logic, even if only in a rudimentary way. A speaker who cannot form a statement or follow an inference has nothing yet to offer a hearer except his own character and personal influence.

Besides, training our speech is not a superficial addition, made later, to a self that is already complete. It is a key part of how each of us becomes able to give meaning to others and to offer meaningful perspectives on decisions or open questions, which is, after all, the goal.

If we aim primarily to train ourselves for self-expression and to find a mature voice, we may take up rhetoric sooner than the default order. That is not because self-expression and finding one's voice come first. It is because part of knowing how best to take a course of study is knowing where that study is going and where it ends; so, before the study begins, we consider the whole course of studies and skills of the trivium. We then return to the best beginning, which is grammar and then logic. The foundation is the same: words first, because teaching and learning are always already done in speech.

What follows begins from ordinary experiences of being misunderstood and of making a promise. If we are still concerned to find a voice for self-expression, we use those experiences as a way of training, honing, and refining the capacity for self-expression.
