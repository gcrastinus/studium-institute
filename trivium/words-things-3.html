Categories and predicables

❧

Some words name one thing, such as Socrates or this dog here. Others can be said of many things, such as man, dog, or animal. A word of the second kind is universal: it can be the predicate of many subjects, because the concept it signifies is one and is found in many things. A universal concept is not a list of individuals; it presents the nature that each of them has. The tradition orders these universal words in two ways, by the categories and by the predicables.

The categories

The categories are the highest kinds of things that can be said of a subject. The first is substance. Primary substance is what is neither said of another nor in another, such as this man or this dog. Secondary substances are the essences of primary substances, such as man or cat; they can be predicated of a subject, whereas this dog here can only be a subject.

The other nine categories are accidents, that is, ways of being in a substance. Aquinas derives them in his commentary on the Metaphysics (V, lect. 9, nn. 889–91). An accident may belong to a substance from its form (quality) or from its matter (quantity), or in relation to something else (relation). It may also belong to a substance from something outside it: as acting (action) or being acted on (passion), and as being in time (when), in place (where), in a certain arrangement of parts (position), or in having something about it, such as clothing (having).

The categories are about real things: they are the most general kinds of what is.

The predicables

The predicables, which come from Porphyry, are the five ways in which something can be said of a subject.

  1. Genus: what is said of many things that differ in kind, as animal is said of man and dog.
  2. Species: what is said of many things that are the same in kind, as man is said of Socrates and Plato.
  3. Difference: what distinguishes one species within a genus, as rational distinguishes man among animals.
  4. Property: what belongs to every member of a species and only to them, but is not its essence, as the capacity for laughter belongs to man.
  5. Accident: what can belong or not belong to a subject without changing what it is, as being tall belongs to some men.

The nine accidents are usually not essential to a thing, and so demonstration, that is, proof from necessary principles, looks chiefly to substance and to genus, species, difference, and property. (Physics is a partial exception, since it studies quantity, motion, and place.)

First and second intentions

The categories and the predicables are not the same kind of thing. The categories are about real things. The predicables are relations that things have only as they are known; the mind makes them, but with a foundation in reality. (This is not the view of Kant, for whom such forms are imposed by the mind.) The tradition calls our concepts of things first intentions and our concepts of the relations things have as known second intentions. For example, white is a first intention, a concept of a color; but white is a species of color uses the second intention species.

Logic is therefore the science of second intentions. That is why genus, species, subject, predicate, statement, and argument are its proper subject.

The deeper question of how universals exist, in things and in the mind, is the one Porphyry declined to settle at the opening of the Isagoge. This introduction also leaves it for later study.
