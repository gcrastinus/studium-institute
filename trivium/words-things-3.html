Foundation · 6 of 6

One name, many things

❧

Recall the child who said dog at a mastiff. The name reached beyond the room because a name of that sort is made to do so. It is worth seeing exactly how, because this is where the doctrine already set out begins to apply.

Universal and singular

Some words can be said truly of many subjects, and some of only one. Andres draws the line and gives the test:

“Spot is a dog.” “Fido is a dog.” It is possible that both of these statements are true because the word ‘dog’ is a universal. That is, ‘dog’ is one word that can be the predicate for more than one subject.
Anthony Andres, Introduction to Aristotelian Logic, Lesson Two.

Fido is singular; it is meant for one dog. Dog is universal, and so are white and animal. Andres notes the pattern: the more universal the word, the more things it names. Animal names Socrates, Fido, and a killer whale.

This is what the child had: not a list of the dogs there are, but a name that stands to many, so that a dog never met is already covered by it. It is tempting to explain this by saying that the word is shorthand for a very large collection, and Maritain says plainly why that is the wrong account:

This alone suffices to show that a universal concept is anything but a collection of individuals: It applies to an infinite multitude because it is originally one (in the mind). That which it presents immediately to the mind is not a collection or series of individuals, but the nature realized in each one of them.
Jacques Maritain, Formal Logic, Section 2, on the extension of a concept.

The order is the reverse of the shorthand account. The name does not reach many dogs by having been applied to them one at a time; it reaches many because what it means directly is one, and that one is the nature the dogs share. Maritain gives the second temptation its name: nominalism confounds the extension of a concept with its resolution into a simple collection of individuals.

Two ways of measuring a concept, taken as what is known, follow from this, and they are worth knowing because the sets of exercises use them. A concept’s comprehension is what it contains, that is, what belongs to being a dog. Its extension is what it applies to, namely, the dogs. As comprehension grows, extension shrinks: animal applies to more things than dog, and says less about each. Comprehension and extension are themselves relations that the concept has as known.

The same name, and not the same meaning

The first page of this foundation described an argument that turned on a word nobody would define, in which two people were using one sound for two things and calling it a debate. That situation has a name and a diagnosis.

Andres, following the opening of the Categories, distinguishes three ways a name gets used:

  • Univocally, when the things named have the same name and the same account of what they are. Socrates is a man and Plato is a man; man means the same both times.
  • Equivocally, when they have the same name but the account differs. If we call Socrates a man, and also call a statue of Socrates a man, the word has two different functions.
  • Denominatively, when a name is taken from another name with the ending changed, and given to whatever has that characteristic. A man is called virtuous from virtue.

The quarrel was a case of equivocation that had not been diagnosed. Both parties had the sound, but neither had checked whether it carried one account or two. Nothing about the sound itself will tell them, because the sound is the part we assigned. The check has to be made at the level of the concept, which means that it has to be made in words, deliberately, by asking what is meant.

This is also why a definition is the work of the first act of the mind, the grasp of what something is, and not an academic formality. To define is to state the one account that a name is carrying, so that the name can be used the same way twice.

The sets of exercises drill all of this: the predicables, the categories, and division and definition in Ars Syllogistica, and signification in Ars Grammatica. Supposition (what a term stands for in a given proposition) is a property of terms in logic; Ars Grammatica names the distinction and passes it on, and Ars Syllogistica is its home. These distinctions are named here and not developed further.

On not settling everything here

There is a deeper question standing behind universals, and many readers will already have sensed it. If dog names many dogs by way of one concept, what is the standing of that one? Is it something real, or only something in our minds? And if it is real, is it in the dogs, or apart from them?

Those are the three questions Porphyry raises at the opening of the Isagoge, which was the standard introduction to this material for something like fifteen centuries. This is what he does with them:

I shall keep away from the deeper questions but shall aim rightly at the simpler, i.e., I shall refuse to say whether genus and species are subsistent or are located only in naked concepts, and if subsistent, whether they are corporeal or incorporeal, and whether separate from sensible or subsisting in them and around them. That business is very deep and requires a greater examination.
Porphyry, Isagoge, opening, as printed in Andres.

He declines them in his first paragraph and gets on with the work. Andres does the same and says so: these are difficult questions, and we will postpone them until we are further along in philosophy. Maritain, at the corresponding place, assigns the formation of the mental word to psychology rather than settling it in a logic.

That dog is a species is a relation the nature has as known. How the nature itself exists in the dogs and in the mind is the deeper question that Porphyry declines.

This course observes the same limit. Everything in this account is settled doctrine, and it is needed before one can begin. The question of the inner word, left open on the first of them, is of the other kind, and it is left open by better books than this one. Declining a question at the threshold is not a gap in an introduction; it is how introductions are built, and Porphyry set the precedent.

The tools of the mind

One last point, so that the three arts are in view before we take up the first of them: Andres compares the mind to the hand.

The hand is a universal tool; it can do nearly anything, but it does few things well. We can dig with our hands, and fight with them, but it is better to dig with a shovel. So the hand’s real advantage is that it makes tools, and with them does those tasks well.

The mind is like that. Left to itself, it manages ordinary matters well enough; no one needs logic to learn to fix a car. On harder questions, however, it does poorly unless it makes tools for itself, and the tools it makes are these: words that carry a thought precisely, statements that put words together, and arguments that put statements together. Grammar, logic, and rhetoric are the care of those tools.

The word is ours, conventional, and replaceable; the concept is not ours and is measured by the thing; and the statement is where truth becomes possible. Some of what logic names is formed by the mind among things as known; it is not invented, and it is not in the things apart from our knowing. The first art is the composing of the first of these into the third.
