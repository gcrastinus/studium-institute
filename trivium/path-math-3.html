Why Words Come First

Mathematics has a more limited domain than language, since language can be used to consider everything and mathematics is restricted to the formal or quantitative aspects of things. Mathematical notation is similar to language in that, typically, the notation has little connection to what an expression in the symbolic system is about; for example, in 3 + 4 = 7, the symbols for the numbers are conventional. But mathematical symbols have a clear and determinate subject matter, unlike language, which can treat of everything. So, language can describe mathematics and calculation, but not vice versa.

Language, mathematical symbols, diagrams, and drawings all put our thought into a form that we can experience with our senses. For example, we may try to solve an equation in our head and then realize that we need to write it down or say it out loud, and doing that makes it easier for us to work through it. But compared to other aids like diagrams or mathematical symbols, language has three advantages for representing our thought: first, language depends entirely on what is natural to every normal human, namely mouth, tongue, ears, and intellectual capacity, and is therefore always possible and near at hand for everyone; second, sounds produced by the mouth can be quite varied, and almost none of them has an intrinsic connection, as sound to the things we want to describe in our words; and, third, language has a universal scope and can be used to represent anything, far beyond the expressive power even of the most advanced mathematics.

Diagrams and drawings can, of course, be quite helpful for various things. But even those diagrams that we do make are almost always things that we make sense of with language, since we place names and labels on different parts of the diagram and often describe in words what the different parts mean. In the same way, when a mathematical question or problem cannot be solved or an impasse is reached in the given formulation, we can always express the problem in language and consider it at length in more general terms.

This also has an important implication for mathematics: it can never be a complete system because it relies on words and concepts beyond itself, terms and principles common to other disciplines.

Nothing here takes away from mathematics; mathematical tools and mathematics itself are extremely powerful for understanding the world. The point is just that language is more general and more powerful than mathematics and that it grounds mathematical thinking. That is why this course begins with the arts of language, that is, grammar, logic, and rhetoric, rather than with mathematics and mathematical representations.
