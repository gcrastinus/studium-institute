Self-Expression as a Starting Point

We are not wrong to want to begin our course of higher studies with learning to express ourselves well. People do need to speak in their own person, to learn to be themselves, and to take positions as their own. A classroom that only corrects grammar and spelling and never asks what is being said will not reach the goal that any education should aim at, namely, that the person himself should be transformed and finish his studies self-possessed in mind and opinions. The modern emphasis on self-expression and voice notices something real: speech is an act of someone, addressed to someone, about something that matters to them.

It is also true that a student who is afraid to speak needs more than a scheme of arguments and lessons in how words have meaning. The confidence to stand and speak is also part of the art of rhetoric, not an addition to it. Sincerity, too, is something students must find, although primarily by themselves; and an audience can tell when a speaker is only performing, which does not build trust in the speaker or in his competence on the subject.

The desire to “find a voice to express oneself and worry about language later, if at all” often comes from having been trained in correctness without anyone ever asking the student what he or she thinks, and without a place to inquire into anything in a genuine way. Where school was like that, the concern is fair. An education in speech that never reaches something a student cares about, such as a claim, a story, or a plea, has not yet taught what speech truly is or what it is meant to be.

What is true, then, is this: we need to speak as ourselves, and it is not enough to write correctly in a mindless or disconnected way. What has not yet been shown is that we can somehow express ourselves before words, or that we can find our own voice without studying grammar, logic, and rhetoric.
