Foundation · 3 of 6

Discourse, and the three arts

❧

We already know from experience what it is for the mind to move. We follow a recipe from flour to dough, a story from a name to a deed, and an explanation from what we knew to what we did not. That movement is discourse: the movement of the mind from one consideration to another for the sake of understanding something. It is not a special academic activity but the ordinary activity of a human mind when it is trying to understand. The most general and powerful instrument of discourse is the expression of thought in language, that is, in sentences and words.

If language is that instrument, then to get better at discourse we must know how to use language well, and to use an instrument well we must understand it. The words we use about a thing are not an afterthought to inquiry; they are how the inquiry proceeds, how it is checked, and how it is communicated. The study of discourse in general must therefore begin with the study of language itself.

We examine our words directly in two ways: first, as to their correctness and adequacy for expressing what we are thinking; and, second, as to how adequate they are to the things we are thinking about. Both questions are already present in the experiences already considered: the failed lesson, the undefined word in a quarrel, and the law whose wording has to be taken as it stands. Consider what this pair of examinations implies. If a word can be measured against a thought and against a thing, then a word, a thought, and a thing are three, not two. How the three stand to one another is taken up next.

The second of those two examinations, adequacy to things, is taken up on the three pages that follow, which draw on Aristotle’s On Interpretation with Aquinas’s commentary, on Porphyry, and on modern teachers of the same material: Walshe, Andres, Houser, and Maritain. Where those sources leave something open, it is marked as open.

The classical name for the arts that care for this instrument is the trivium. In the classical and medieval world, the studies of these three, language, logic, and persuasion, made up the trivium, and they laid out for students the foundations of how we come to express ourselves, to show that something is true, and to persuade others of that truth, foundations on which all other disciplines are built.

This course uses the school names. Grammar concerns what is said and how speech is composed; logic concerns how statements follow logically from one another; and rhetoric concerns how speech moves a hearer toward assent. They are taken up one by one. Each is a short account, followed by practice in the matching set of exercises, and none is a substitute for the arts themselves.

The default next step is grammar. Logic works on statements, and a statement is made of words; rhetoric offers speech to a hearer, and there is no speech yet if nothing has been said. If a statement cannot yet be distinguished from a question or a command, the opening of logic may be taken up sooner; that case is rare, and the account of grammar says so. For everyone else, the first art is the care of significant speech, that is, speech that signifies or means something.

Before the arts themselves, there is one more foundation. All three arts handle words, and so it is worth knowing what function a word has when it reaches a thing.
