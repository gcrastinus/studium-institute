Why mathematics needs words

❧

One might object to the classical trivium's claim that words must come first by arguing that the highest truths are scientific theories, that these are stated in mathematical form, and that mathematics is therefore the greatest instrument of the human mind for articulating important truths. But on careful consideration it is clear that mathematical expressions do not have meaning in themselves, except in so far as that meaning is given by language; and mathematical expressions certainly cannot interpret themselves. For example, a simple expression of one of the laws of physics may be given as a formula, but the formula itself is incomplete until we have been told what the terms mean, that is, for example, that if F is force and m is mass and a is acceleration, then Newton’s Second Law of motion relating force applied, the body's mass, and its resulting acceleration, can be expressed as F = ma.

It is true that the ease of use of the notation and its manipulation make forms and equations like F = ma convenient. Such forms and equations can even be taught to students, without necessarily requiring that they understand the expression fully. But that should not obscure the fact that a notation is useless without the correct interpretation, and any such interpretation is and must be given in language.

The same holds for mathematics as a whole. The fundamental definitions and results of mathematics are articulated in primarily linguistic form, with definitions and axioms; and even for those articulated primarily in symbolic form, it is always the case that the symbols are given meaning by definitions in language. This is why mathematical formulae can always be read out loud. Likewise, advanced symbolic systems are learned typically both through instruction given in a language and through examples of the use of the system, and learners often find it necessary to express to themselves in language what the symbolic system means.

This is true even of the most general part of modern mathematics, mathematical logic. Mathematical logic is constructed around a formal language where terms and rules are precisely defined; but the articulation and elaboration of these terms and rules can only be done in a natural language, in what is called the metalanguage of the system (usually English). This means that even the most general and powerful tools of mathematics must be expressed and defined in language.

Language is, therefore, necessary, even if it is not sufficient, for the articulation of mathematics and for the ordering of mathematical thought.
