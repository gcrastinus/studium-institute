Path · critical thinking

From thinking back to speech

❧

Logic, as the trivium treats it, is the art of ordering discourse, the movement of the mind from one thing known to another, toward truth. It presupposes significant speech: terms that mean, statements that can be true or false, questions and commands that are not statements, and paragraphs that convince an audience of something true. We cannot follow an argument or an attempt to persuade unless that argument or effort has been put into language.

What about images? We sometimes speak of images as having a rhetoric or a discourse, or as being able to persuade people of something. This is true, of course, but only figuratively: an image cannot itself persuade us of anything; it is what the image seems to imply that may persuade us when we understand it in the right context. Imagine, for example, a candid photograph of a famous politician caught doing something terrible; he has seen the camera just before the picture was taken and is reaching toward it to block it. The image itself is damning, but only because we understand the context, know the politician, and know that what he was caught doing is wrong.

It is words, then, that communicate, speak truth, and persuade.

Most readers here will begin with grammar. The reason is simply that we must first be able to speak and hear correctly, and to know what is meant by our words, before we can ask in detail whether what the words say is true or not.

If we want to begin with inference and logic, that is a good desire. Nothing in these foundations asks us to become less strict; they ask us to become more strict about speech, since speech is where inference and discourse are always found. Ars Syllogistica trains us in inference. But it is wise not to neglect the preliminary study, even though it is not a difficult one.

Next, we begin from ordinary experiences of being misunderstood, of teaching a name, of arguing, and of reading a law. The art of inference belongs after that; it does not replace grammar.
