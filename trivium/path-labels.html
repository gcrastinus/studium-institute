Path · labels

How words might lead us astray

❧

Things are what we want to know: the natures of things, their properties, their causes, and so on. A child pointing at a bird does not point primarily for love of the word “bird.” The farmer, the nurse, the mechanic, and the astronomer have to deal with what is there, with things. If words were merely decorative, we would be right to see no reason to consider them first or in detail.

The modern suspicion of “mere words” often comes from having watched words and language used to hide things, whether actively or passively, by cloaking them in words that obscure an important reality of some kind. Slogans, jargon, and official language that will not look at the object can all lead us to think that a concern for words and definitions is “just semantics.” Sometimes that is quite true: a dispute that is only about the label itself, while the thing is ignored, is not a serious dispute.

It is also true that we can get lost in definitions and never return to what we were trying to know. It is always a temptation to consider words rather than to study things themselves; this is especially true of modern historically minded disciplines, where the concern can be primarily with the words that others in the past have used for the things in question. Anyone who has sat through a conference or a class of that kind can see how words can be a distraction from reality.

Furthermore, we may sometimes be concerned about beginning with words because we think that words have no stable meaning, or that what we can express in words is somehow merely subjective; we may even be led to think that words in different languages can be so different that some peoples could never understand each other. If this were true, whether of words, of expressions, or of whole languages, it would be equally true of any other symbolic system we might hope to use. Likewise, if languages were so different that the speakers of one could not understand the speakers of another, it would be equally impossible for those peoples to learn from each other or to build anything together; some languages would, in a certain sense, be impenetrable to others, or completely or partially uninterpretable. But it is false that speakers of one language cannot learn from those of another, and it is a fact of experience that there are no uninterpretable languages.

What is right in this skepticism about the primacy of speech, however, is the centrality of the thing as what we are trying to know, together with the simple observation that considering speech as such will not by itself be sufficient for considering the thing in question.
