How Words Might Lead Us Astray

The desire to begin from things rather than words is not a mistake. Things are what we want to know: the natures of things, their properties, their causes, and so on. A child pointing at a bird does not point primarily for love of the word “bird.” The farmer, the nurse, the mechanic, and the astronomer all have to deal with what is there, with things. If words were merely decorative, we would be right to see no reason to consider them first or in detail.

The modern suspicion of “mere words” often comes from having seen language used to hide things, whether deliberately or not, by words that obscure some important reality. Slogans, jargon, and official language that does not attend to its object can all lead us to think that a concern for words and definitions is “just semantics.” Sometimes that is true: a dispute that is only about the label, while the thing itself is ignored, is not a serious dispute.

It is also true that we can become lost in definitions and never return to what we were trying to know. It is always a temptation to consider words rather than to study things themselves; this is especially true of modern historically minded disciplines, where the concern can be primarily with the words that others in the past have used for the things in question. Anyone who has attended a conference or a class of that kind knows how words can distract us from reality.

We may also hesitate to begin with words because we think that words have no stable meaning, or that what we can express in words is merely subjective; we may even think that languages can be so different that some peoples could never understand each other. But if this were true of words, of expressions, or of whole languages, it would be equally true of any other symbolic system we might hope to use. Likewise, if the speakers of one language could not understand the speakers of another, it would be impossible for those peoples to learn from each other or to build anything together, since some languages would be completely or partially uninterpretable to others. But it is false that speakers of one language cannot learn from those of another, and it is a fact of experience that there are no uninterpretable languages.

What is true, then, is this: the thing is what we are trying to know, and considering speech as such will not by itself be sufficient for knowing the thing in question. What has not yet been shown is that we can know and teach things without first attending to the words by which we speak of them.
