Why we begin with words

❧

We have all been misunderstood. We said one thing; someone heard something else; and whatever we were doing together either stopped or went astray after that, with each party thinking the other meant something other than he did. This is common throughout human life, whether in the sciences or between managers and employees.

Consider a mother teaching a name to a child. The child saw a bird, and her mother gave her the word for it. Before she knew the name, the child did not know what to call it, and so she could not ask questions about it or easily keep track of it over time. The child did not receive a physical bird from her mother; she received a way to speak about birds, to investigate them, and to relate them to other things.

Imagine an argument in which the participants go back and forth, each arguing for what he thinks to be true, and then discover that the whole argument comes down to one word that they have not yet defined, or a word that they define differently. They were not, then, disagreeing about things but about words: they were either using one word for two things or two words for one thing. Until both have the same understanding of what the word means, they cannot make much progress.

Laws take the form of words, and through them we know what we are allowed to do and what we may not do, what we must do and what will take place in certain circumstances. Words and their meanings are therefore crucial for the political and legal order, to say nothing of the wider social world.

Promises too take the form of words, and through them our whole lives can be altered, from the promises made in marriage to the promise a soldier makes to serve his country for a set number of years. The same is true of prayers and petitions, of the exhortations a leader sometimes has to make, and of the sentence a judge passes on a guilty man.

All our learning and all our theory begin from the words we use, what they mean, and what we do with them. A community of learners cannot even consider methodology, principles, or an approach to a set of questions before it has a common language and a common way of understanding language, meaning, and truth, at least implicitly. The later sciences, the later arts, and even the later piety of a tradition all come about within the broad order of speech, words, meaning, and truth.

This is also confirmed historically: in every major classical culture, from China and India to the Ancient Near East and Greece, the first disciplines to be discovered and articulated, and the disciplines that guide the others, were all based on language, meaning, and truth, that is, on names and terms, on sentences and texts.

In insisting that the best way to begin is with words, the classical tradition of the trivium does not treat our private thoughts as things that exist without language and that we later merely express in words, or not, as we wish. Rather, the trivium begins from the fact that teaching, learning, promising, arguing, giving judgment, and articulating our thoughts in general are all necessary foundations for any higher study or cooperative effort. This is true even if we think of ourselves only as individuals; even an individual's own thoughts take form in words, as will be shown.

The claim that language is or could be completely private, so that each speaker first has his own thoughts without reference to language and only later comes to express them in words, is much closer to the modern tradition of philosophical speculation on the mind, as, for example, in the work of thinkers like René Descartes and Francis Bacon. The account given by the classical trivium is outlined in How a word reaches a thing.

How words, concepts, and things actually relate to one another is a real question, but it is not the first question. The first question is simpler: What is the purpose or goal of language? And does it have more than one?
