The Purposes of Language

❧

Language has two purposes naturally: it is used for communication and as the principal instrument for aiding human thought. The first is obvious and well-known; the second is no less important but harder to see. We will explore both. Getting clear on these two purposes and how they relate will lay the foundations for everything that follows.

Humans as social animals

Humans are social animals. We cannot live completely alone. We have no natural protections against the weather and no parts of the body naturally suited to attack or defend. Our primary ways of protecting ourselves from the weather and predators, as well as our primary ways of obtaining food are all based on collective common effort; so, in that way, humans are naturally the most social of all animals. The early modern picture of humans as naturally free of society, later entering a social contract, was a false picture.

Social animals and communication

Social animals by definition work together, and to collaborate they must be able to communicate. Animals pursue benefits and avoid harms for themselves and their group; so, that is what animals communicate about. An animal will only use communication that is possible for it, relevant for its life, and about benefits and harms. As a related aside, we might wonder how we can know the degree to which animals themselves have elaborate thought that we do not perceive. Although it is clear that animals must decide when and how to do things and to a degree even solve certain problems related to the conduct of their lives, nevertheless, it is clear that they do not have elaborate internal thought for four reasons:

  1. Nothing in their behavior shows advanced thought, beyond what we would expect them to need to solve the problems they face.
  2. They have no need to spend scarce energy on elaborate internal thought that gives them no clear benefit.
  3. The one animal that does think gives that thought physical form in speech and language, namely humankind.
  4. Unexpressed thought would bring them no other benefit, since animals are very capable of solving their problems without the hypothesis of elaborate internal thought.

Some animal communication is innate and some is learned, as in the case of nightingales that teach songs to their young and humpback whale songs that can spread throughout a whole ocean region when males imitate one another.

Communication with universal scope: human language

Communication within a human social group is the primary purpose of language. Human communication in language is the expression of thought in a public way so that others may understand what has been expressed. Humans communicate about what is relevant to human life, and the sphere of human concern is universal and general, from food and shelter to knowing the stars and understanding the basic forces of matter. So, what human linguistic acts can be about is also universal in scope. Humans can also encode communication into practically any physical form and even invent entirely new systems of communication. This is all a sign that there is something in human nature beyond language: the human capacity for thought.

Language as the principal instrument for human thought

Since we lack natural defenses and live by thinking and understanding, and since thinking correctly is difficult and understanding is the result of effort, we need an instrument that can refine, extend, or clarify our thinking. Reflecting on our thoughts purely by mental effort is difficult, because thoughts are fleeting and hard to grasp. Language expresses our thoughts in particular and fixed words, which gives them a more stable form that we can experience with our senses and also remember more easily. We can then evaluate whether the words adequately express our thoughts and whether those thoughts adequately capture the reality in question. At the highest level, we require language to articulate the foundations and highest results of our inquiries: definitions, theories, and discoveries must be stated to be understood; and that means they must be linguistic in form.

Compared to other instruments of thought like diagrams or mathematical symbols, language has three advantages:

  1. It depends entirely on what is natural to every normal human.
  2. Its sounds are extremely varied and almost none has an intrinsic connection to things.
  3. It has a universal scope.

Many traditions, from Confucius, Panini, and Aristotle to Von Humboldt and modern cognitive science, have held that language is the principal and habitual instrument for aiding human thought. But where does language come from? It must come from the human mind, in particular many minds working together. Even if left entirely alone and without any outside linguistic influence, groups of children invent their own language, as, for example, the well-known case of deaf orphans in Nicaragua.

The two purposes ordered to one goal

Language as an instrument of thought presupposes language as expression of thought. By language we express and communicate our thought and also refine and perfect it. Both are ordered to another goal beyond themselves: we communicate our newly refined thought to others to contribute to our community as social animals. This is the highest good of communication and thought.

To study discourse we must begin by studying language

This is the central truth around which this whole account is built:

  • Discourse is the movement of the mind from one consideration to another for the sake of understanding something.
  • The most general and powerful instrument for discourse is the expression of thought in language.
  • To get better at discourse, we must know how to use language well.
  • To use an instrument in the best way, the instrument itself must be understood.

Words and Thoughts

We examine our words in two ways: as adequate to what we are thinking, and as adequate to the things we are thinking about. Discourse in language is the highest form that human thought can take. We put order into our thoughts by putting order into our words. So we must first study words in general: how words have meanings, what meaning is, what kinds of words there are, and how words relate to particular languages.
