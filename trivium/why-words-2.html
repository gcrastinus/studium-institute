What language is for

❧

We speak in order to be understood. That is so ordinary that it can go unnoticed. But we also speak in order to think: we try a sentence aloud, we write a clause to see whether we meant it, and we keep a name in mind so that a thought will not be forgotten. Language, then, serves two purposes at once, and we already practice both of them.

Language has, by nature, two purposes: it is used for communication, and it is the principal instrument that aids human thought.

The first purpose is the one everyone grants. We live with others, and we cannot do the work of a human life (raising children, making a living, keeping the peace, handing on a craft) without making our thought available to someone else. We are social animals, and coordination requires communication. Human communication in language is the expression of thought in a public way, so that others may understand what has been expressed.

The second purpose is easier to miss. Thought is fleeting; it changes and reverses itself, and it will not remain fixed long enough to be examined. When we put a thought into words that we can hear or see, whether spoken, written, or remembered as a sentence, we give it a form to which we can return. Then we can ask two questions that are almost impossible to ask of a thought that has not been put into words: Did I say what I was thinking? And does what I said fit the thing I meant to think about?

Other aids serve some of the same purposes: a diagram, a column of numbers, a sketch. But language is the aid always available, and the one that can be about anything we can consider. Definitions, which every serious inquiry needs, are linguistic, and results, when they are to be kept and taught, are stated. Even the student who wants to begin from data is already using this instrument whenever the data become a report.

The two purposes are not opposed. We refine thought by expressing it, often as if to another person, and then we offer the refined thought to others because we live with them. We also receive most of what we come to understand through words, from parents, teachers, and those further along in a practice than we are. A community of learners is, in that sense, a community of speakers, and for both reasons it has to care for names, statements, and speeches.
