The Purposes of Language

❧

Language has two purposes naturally: it is used for communication and as the principal instrument for aiding human thought. The first is obvious and well-known; the second is no less important but harder to see. We will explore both. Getting clear on these two purposes and how they relate will lay the foundations for everything that follows.

Humans as social animals

Humans are social animals. We cannot live completely alone. We have no natural protections against the weather and no parts of the body naturally suited to attack or defend. Our primary protection, defenses, hunting, and finding food are all based on collective common effort, so humans are naturally the most social of all animals. Complete solitude is a punishment and can lead to insanity. The early modern picture of humans as naturally free of society, later entering a social contract, was a false picture. The exploration those same movements sped up confirmed in a definitive way that humankind is naturally social.

Social animals and communication

Social animals by definition work together, and to collaborate they must be able to communicate. Animals pursue benefits and avoid harms for themselves and their group, so that is what animals communicate about: wolves howl, bees dance, dolphins seem to have something like names. An animal will only use communication that is possible for it, relevant for its life, and about benefits and harms. Animals are not thinking internally in elaborate ways, for four reasons:

  1. Nothing in their behavior shows advanced thought.
  2. They have no need to spend scarce energy on it.
  3. The one animal that does think gives that thought physical form in speech and language.
  4. Unexpressed thought would bring them no other benefit.

Some animal communication is innate and some is learned, as with Aristotle's nightingales, humpback whale songs, and crows.

Communication with universal scope: human language

Communication within a human social group is the primary purpose of language. Human communication in language is the expression of thought in a public way so that others may understand what has been expressed. Humans communicate about what is relevant to human life, and the sphere of human concern is universal and general. Any proposed counterexample becomes, by being proposed, an object of human concern. So what human linguistic acts can be about is also universal in scope. Humans can also encode communication into practically any physical form and invent entirely new systems of communication. There is something in human nature beyond language: the human capacity for thought.

Language as the principal instrument for human thought

Language is something physical that we have made for a purpose. Since we lack natural defenses and live by thinking and understanding, and since our thought is difficult and understanding is the result of effort, we need an instrument that can refine, extend, or clarify our thinking. Reflecting on our thoughts purely by mental effort is difficult, because thoughts are fleeting and hard to grasp. Language expresses our thoughts in particular and fixed words, which gives them a more stable form that we can experience with our senses. We can then evaluate whether the words adequately express our thoughts, and whether those thoughts adequately capture the reality. Compared to diagrams or mathematical symbols, language has three advantages:

  1. It depends entirely on what is natural to every normal human.
  2. Its sounds are varied and almost none has an intrinsic connection to things.
  3. It has a universal scope.

We also use language to articulate the foundations and highest results of our inquiries: definitions are and must be linguistic in form. Many traditions, from Confucius, Panini, and Aristotle to Von Humboldt and modern cognitive science, have held that language is the principal and habitual instrument for aiding human thought. The mind, or many minds working together, must be the source of its greatest instrument, as the deaf orphans in Nicaragua show.

The two purposes ordered to one goal

Language as an instrument of thought presumes language as expression of thought. By language we express and communicate our thought and also refine and perfect it. Both are ordered to a goal beyond them: we communicate our newly refined thought to others to contribute to our community as social animals. This is the highest good of communication and thought. Much of our thinking is formed in us through the medium of language first.

To study discourse we must begin by studying language

  • Discourse is the movement of the mind from one consideration to another for the sake of understanding something.
  • The most general and powerful instrument for discourse is the expression of thought in language.
  • To get better at discourse, we must know how to use language well.
  • To use an instrument in the best way, the instrument itself must be understood.

We examine our words in two ways: as adequate to what we are thinking, and as adequate to the things we are thinking about. Discourse in language is the highest form that human thought can take, and its success shows that our words have captured the world as it is, at least in many ways. We put order into our thoughts by putting order into our words, so our study of discourse must be a study of our words, in general, not on a particular topic. So we must first study words in general: how words have meanings, what meaning is, what kinds of words there are, and how words relate to particular languages.
