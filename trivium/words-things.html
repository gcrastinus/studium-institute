Words, Concepts, and Things

The elements of discourse are what we know and the terms which make up what we know. Before we can take up the three arts, we need a straightforward account of how our words relate to our thoughts and to things.

What a thing is

In the classical tradition, we sometimes speak about things and sometimes about beings. What is the difference? A thing (Latin: res) is anything that we can speak about considered as to what it is, that is, as to its essence. Trees, numbers, colors, laws, and associations of lawyers are all things in this sense. When we consider the same things as to whether they exist, we speak instead of a “being”; so, when we consider tree in general or association in general we are considering tree or association as a thing; when we consider this tree here or this particular association, we are considering them as beings. So the word “thing” is quite general; it covers whatever can be named and considered; and logic studies things as known, as to what they are, and not whether this individual here and now exists.

The first operation of the intellect is the grasping of the essence of anything that can be spoken about, that is, the grasping of the essence of a thing (res) as we just defined it.

The concept

When we come to understand something, the mind draws out from our experience what the thing is (the tradition calls this abstraction) and forms within itself a concept of that thing. The concept is the "term" (from the Latin terminus, end) of our act of understanding, that is, the concept is what our act of understanding a thing produces. A concept is also called a “term” in logic because a concept is what stands at each end position of a proposition, namely as subject or predicate in a statement of the form “A is B.” These positions are what define the second act of the mind, the proposition.

Words differ from one language to another, but concepts of the same thing are the same for everyone. English says “cat” where another language might say “billee,” but the concept is one, because both are understandings of the same kind of animal. Compare two photographs of the same thing taken by two people: the cameras differ, but what is shown is the same; this is also true of concepts.

This is a counterintuitive claim in our time, but it is a foundational one for the whole classical tradition. Sometimes two people seem to disagree about a concept when they are really using the same word but using it for two different concepts. If one person means by “faith” a feeling and another means a virtue, they are not yet talking about the same thing. It is true, of course, that one person's concept can be clearer than another person's concept of the same thing, but the central point is that concepts of the same thing are in fact the same. Two concepts are the same formally when they make known the same thing; they can differ in clarity and completeness. This is a feature of being a likeness: some likenesses are clearer and more complete, but that fact does not take away from the fact that other likenesses are also of the given thing.

Concepts are likenesses of things and make us aware of the things they are likenesses of. This is how concepts are about things. But note that concepts do not just happen to be about things. It is not as if the concept of cat could somehow be the concept of something else or the concept of cat could somehow be anything other than a concept. In this way, concepts are like our experiences through the senses: if we see something, that act of seeing could not be anything other than an act of seeing. So, a concept, just by the kind of thing it is, is a likeness of the thing that it is a concept of.

The order of words, concepts, and things

When someone speaks to us, their words, which are sounds carried through the air to our ears, bring to our mind the concepts that those words refer to and those concepts immediately bring to mind the things that those concepts are concepts of. If someone says that the piano is black, those words "piano" and "black" bring to our mind the concepts of piano and the color black and through the concepts of piano and black we have now in mind pianos and the color, which are real things. So, do words refer to things or concepts? Words refer to things through concepts, since a word does not refer to a concept first and primarily but to what the concept is a likeness of. Words, therefore, refer ultimately to things but they do so through the concepts of the mind.

This means that words are signs. Just as smoke is a sign of fire, so too a spoken word which is a physical sound is a sign of a concept in the mind and calls that concept to our awareness. So, words are signs of concepts directly and only through concepts are words signs of things.

But what precisely is a sign?

Philosophical Exploration: Is reference through concepts possible?

Some of the most influential philosophers of the last century attacked the picture on which words name things by way of ideas in the mind. Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations opens by attacking the view that every word is a name of an object and that its meaning is the object or the mental picture of it. Saul Kripke argued that a name such as "Cicero" does not refer to Cicero because the speaker has a description or idea that fits the man; the name, Kripke says, has reference because it passes down a chain of use that goes back to the man Cicero himself. Hilary Putnam pushed the puzzles of reference further when he imagined a Twin Earth where the liquid called "water" is not H₂O. A person there could have exactly the same ideas as we do and still refer to a different substance, and this proves, Putnam argues, that "meanings just ain't in the head."

Much of this is true, and the classical account accepts a great deal of it. What a word refers to is not fixed by a private mental picture alone. A person can use "elm" or "gold" correctly with a vague or even somewhat mistaken concept and rely on those who know the matter. The concept we have of a thing can be unclear and incomplete, and it can be corrected by experience. More importantly, however, words have many uses besides naming; so, contemporary philosophers are right to insist on that.

None of this reaches the classical account, though. These philosophical criticisms are mainly aimed at the early modern view that the ideas themselves are what we know first, i.e. that we know our concepts first, and it is our ideas that we name with our words. But, for the classical tradition, in Aquinas, for example, the concept is that by which we understand a thing, and it is not that which we understand (Summa Theologiae I, q. 85, a. 2); and words signify things, he says, by means of or through our concepts (I, q. 13, a. 1). The concept does not, therefore, stand between us and the thing as an object of its own; our knowledge is not knowledge about our concepts. The concept is a likeness through which the thing itself is made known, and the thing is something that exists in the world itself somehow beyond the mind. That is why two people's concepts can be the same formally while differing in clarity and completeness.

Putnam sought to give an account of how natural kinds work, that is, how things like two bodies of water and two trees can be of the same kind, and in doing so he came close to a classical account here, as Kripke did too. He holds that "water" refers to whatever has the same underlying nature as the water we point to, and that science may discover what that nature is. That is much like saying a kind has an essence which we grasp more or less clearly. The Twin Earth thought experiment shows how incomplete a concept can be. It does not show that there is no common nature that our concepts are tracking.
