How a word reaches a thing

❧

Consider a few ordinary experiences of words. We often use a word for something we have never seen: we can say glacier before we have ever stood at one, and when we finally do, we know what we are looking at. Two people who share no language can point at the same dog and make two different sounds, and neither sound is wrong. And a child who learns the word dog from a spaniel will, a week later, say dog of a mastiff he has never met, and say it correctly, without having been told that mastiffs count.

None of this would be possible if a word were simply attached to a thing. The sounds differ, but the dog does not. The child was not given a list of animals; he was given a name that already reached beyond the animal in the room. So, something stands between the word and the thing, and it has a real part in how the word signifies.

The last page ended with two questions by which we examine our speech: whether our words express what we are thinking, and whether they are adequate to the things we are thinking about. Two such measures imply three terms, not two: the word, the thought, and the thing. This page considers the order among them.

What a sign is

Let us begin with the simplest definition, the one Andres gives when he introduces the vocabulary of logic:

sign — something relative which points out another thing to a knowing power.
Anthony Andres, Introduction to Aristotelian Logic, definitions to Lesson Eight.

The definition has three parts, and each of them matters. First, a sign is relative; strictly speaking, the sign is the relation itself, and the smoke or the word is what carries that relation. Second, a sign points out another thing; what it presents is not itself. Third, it points that thing out to a knowing power; that is, something is a sign only for a knowing power that can grasp it. Smoke over a hill, for example, signifies fire to someone who knows how to read smoke, but to the hill it is only smoke. Even when no one reads it, however, the smoke is still apt to signify: it is a sign in first act, that is, a sign by capacity, although it is not actually signifying.

On this definition, both a word and a concept are signs. They are not, however, the same kind of sign; that difference is the subject of the next page. Here we consider first the order in which they stand.

The order of signification

Aristotle sets out this order in the opening sentences of On Interpretation, the work that stands between the Categories and the logic of argument.

Now those that are in vocal sound are signs of passions in the soul, and those that are written are signs of those in vocal sound. And just as letters are not the same for all men so neither are vocal sounds the same; but the passions of the soul, of which vocal sounds are the first signs are the same for all; and the things of which passions of the soul are likenesses are also the same.
Aristotle, On Interpretation 1, 16a3–8, in the translation printed with Aquinas’s commentary.

“Passions of the soul” is a phrase that can mislead an English reader, and Aquinas pauses to clarify it. It does not mean emotions such as anger or joy. Here it means conceptions of the intellect, that is, what we ordinarily call concepts. Aristotle uses the word passion, Aquinas explains, because a concept arises in us from the thing by a kind of impression; in coming to understand, the mind undergoes something.

So, there are four items, and they stand in an order: writing signifies speech, speech signifies concepts, and concepts are likenesses of things. Andres states the same order without the technical vocabulary and draws out its consequence:

words signify our thoughts about reality. The words may be different in different languages, but they all signify the same kind of thoughts because the thoughts are likenesses of the same reality.
Anthony Andres, Introduction to Aristotelian Logic, Lesson Eight.

This answers the puzzle of the dog and the two different sounds. English and Latin differ at the first two levels of the order, writing and speech, and agree at the last two, concepts and things. The sounds belong to a particular people; what the sounds signify does not.

Why a word cannot skip the concept

It might seem simpler if words named things directly. Aquinas, however, gives an argument that they cannot.

Therefore “passions in the soul” must be understood here as conceptions of the intellect, and names, verbs, and speech, signify these conceptions of the intellect immediately according to the teaching of Aristotle. They cannot immediately signify things, as is clear from the mode of signifying, for the name “man” signifies human nature in abstraction from singulars; hence it is impossible that it immediately signify a singular man.
Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Aristotle’s On Interpretation, Book I, Lesson 2.

Consider what the argument turns on. The word man does not mean this man, nor does it mean the collection of all men; we did not have to meet all men in order to learn the word. It means human nature, taken apart from any particular bearer. But there is no such thing existing on its own in the world; outside the mind, every man is some particular man. Plato placed the abstraction outside the mind and so arrived at a separate Form; Aristotle does not. So, if the word means something general, and the world contains only particulars, then what the word means directly is in the mind, and it is the concept that reaches the thing.

Hence the point can be stated in a single formula: vocal sounds signify the conceptions of the intellect immediately, and things by means of them. Houser uses the same formula as a section heading in his own textbook: Language Signifies Thought, Which in Turn Signifies Things. Andres and Walshe both state the same point as a description of what the logician works on, namely, words insofar as they signify things through our thoughts.

This is not a retreat into the mind

If this order is stated carelessly, it can sound as though we were shut inside our own minds, exchanging concepts and never reaching things. In the same passage, however, the tradition says the opposite. Concepts are likenesses of things, and Aquinas states explicitly that the causality runs from the thing to the concept:

passion is from the impression of something acting, and hence passions of the soul have their origin from things.
Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Aristotle’s On Interpretation, Book I, Lesson 2.

Still, the worry deserves a careful answer, because it is easy to move from “the word means a concept” to “so what I know is my own concept.” Maritain prevents this move with a distinction. (An object, in the older sense of the term, is a thing as known, that is, a reality set before a knowing power.)

First, we can take the concept as something produced: something the mind forms within itself in order to understand. Maritain calls this the mental concept, and the tradition also calls it the mental word. Of the mental concept he says:

it is a pure means, THAT IN WHICH we attain the thing or object, “id in quo intelligimus rem.”
Jacques Maritain, Formal Logic, Section 2, “Notion of the Concept.”

Second, we can take the concept as what is understood by means of it, that is, the nature or essence presented to the mind; this he calls the objective concept. The key sentence is this:

it is essential to distinguish between these two accepted meanings of the word “concept,” and to remember that the “objective concept” is not that by which or in which we know but, on the contrary, it is THAT WHICH we know.
Jacques Maritain, Formal Logic, Section 2, “The Mental Concept and the Objective Concept.”

So, the mental concept is not an object that we inspect in place of the thing; it is that by which we see the thing. Compare Houser’s analogy of a bear seeing a salmon: the process in the bear’s eyes is that by which the salmon is seen, but what the bear looks at is the salmon. A concept, the formal sign properly speaking, is a pure means in the same way. What we know is the thing itself, presented to the mind under some aspect; we are not, then, shut inside our own minds.

This is also why the passage can say that concepts are the same for all men: not because everyone happens to agree, but because a concept takes its measure from what is there. Of the three terms in the order, only the first, the word, is ours to invent.

Aquinas also explains why the middle item in the order, the concept, had to be made known to others:

Now if man were by nature a solitary animal the passions of the soul by which he was conformed to things so as to have knowledge of them would be sufficient for him; but since he is by nature a political and social animal it was necessary that his conceptions be made known to others. This he does through vocal sound.
Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Aristotle’s On Interpretation, Book I, Lesson 2.

The same conclusion follows from our being social animals: language exists because knowing alone is not enough for a creature that must live with others.

What is ours, and what is not

Only one item in the order is our own invention, and that is the word. Aristotle makes this point, and makes it carefully, when he defines the noun.

By a noun we mean a sound significant by convention, which has no reference to time, and of which no part is significant apart from the rest. The limitation “by convention” was introduced because nothing is by nature a noun or name — it is only so when it becomes a sign; inarticulate sounds, such as those which brutes produce, are significant, yet none of these constitutes a noun.
Aristotle, On Interpretation 2, 16a19–29, Edghill translation, as printed in Andres.

A dog’s whine signifies, but it is not a word, because no one assigned it. Words are assigned, or fixed by use; that is why there are many languages, why a new term can be coined, and why a definition can be made more precise. Astronomers, for example, have changed what they mean by planet, and in doing so they changed nothing at all about planets. The word was theirs to fix; what it takes to be a planet was not.

So, to the objection “aren’t words arbitrary?” the answer is that the sounds are arbitrary, but nothing else in the order is.

A related question is left open here, and it is worth saying what kind of question it is. The mental concept is also called the mental word (Latin: verbum mentis), because, as Maritain puts it, the mind utters it within itself. How that uttering takes place, that is, what the intellect does in forming the word and how the word stands to the public language we learned as children, is a question about the mind rather than about logic. Maritain assigns it to psychology; Aquinas, in the same passage quoted above, assigns it to the study of the soul. Nothing on these three pages depends on how it is answered. The third page explains why declining a question at the threshold of a study is ordinary practice and not evasion.
