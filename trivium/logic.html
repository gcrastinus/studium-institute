The second art

Logic as a liberal art

❧

We have all felt something follow logically, and we have all been taken in by something that only appeared to follow logically. A proof in geometry closed; a news claim did not, though it had the shape of a proof; two people used “not” as if they had contradicted each other, when they had only disagreed. Logic begins in such recognitions. It is not a bag of puzzles, and it is not a personality trait called “being critical.” It is the art of ordering discourse toward truth. It is also a science: logic studies the relations things have only because they are known, namely, genus, species, subject, predicate, statement, and conclusion. These are called second intentions.

Discourse is the movement of the mind from one consideration to another for the sake of understanding something. That happens when we go from many things of a kind to what makes the kind what it is; when we relate two concepts in something we take to be true; and when, from what we already know, the mind moves to something we did not know before. These are not three separate pursuits; they are one work, taken at three depths.

R. E. Houser, using the school names that Ars Syllogistica also uses, calls these the three acts of the mind. The first is apprehension, that is, grasping what a term means, which tends toward a definition. The second is judgment, joining or dividing in a proposition, which can be true or false. The third is reasoning, putting propositions together so that a conclusion follows. Pierre Conway analyses the same sequence as the stages of the reasoning process: from the grasp of a nature, through the proposition, to the movement from known to unknown. This lesson takes that order as a map; it does not replace the drills.

Act of the mindWhat is formedWhat is at stake
ApprehensionTerm, definitionWhat is meant
JudgmentPropositionTrue or false
ReasoningArgumentWhether it follows

The second act is where truth first becomes possible, and that is not a technicality. A term signifies without yet asserting: man and white, on their own, are neither true nor false. Only when the mind joins or divides, affirming this of that or denying it, does what is said become the sort of thing that can be so or not so. That is why the first act aims at definition and not at truth, and why a student who wants to argue before he can define is trying to perform the third act without the support of either of the first two.

Opposition belongs to the second act. Two statements may contradict each other, or be contraries, or stand in other relations on the square; they may also merely disagree without being logical opposites at all. Inference belongs to the second act in its immediate form (what follows from one statement alone) and to the third act when a middle term carries us from premises to a conclusion. The practice application will teach the moods and the diagrams. What is needed here is the question those tools serve: given what has been said, what must be said, what cannot be said, and what is still open?

Not everything that looks like an inference is one. Conway’s definition is the one to keep in view when a paragraph has the gait of an argument and none of its necessity.

The definition of a fallacy is that it is reasoning which appears to conclude but does not. The accent is on appears.
Pierre Conway, Aristotelian Formal and Material Logic, Chapter 1.

Conway then states what makes this art urgent rather than decorative: the fallacies a student can name are not the ones that will deceive him.

The important thing to note about fallacies is that they do not seem to be fallacies. Usually when we refer to fallacies, we refer to what appear, at least to us, to be blatant examples of misreasoning. But such are mere second-rate or amateur fallacies. The perfect or expert fallacy is one which looks like, not a fallacy, but, to all but the most trained eye, the most orthodox of reasoning.
Pierre Conway, Aristotelian Formal and Material Logic, Chapter 1.

He draws the public consequence without hesitation: because a known starting point may be believed without being true, “politicians can be convincing even when their premises are false… one cannot say that the truth will necessarily prevail in public discussion.” That is the case for learning this art, and it is not a case about winning debates. It is about being able to tell, in a paragraph that sounds like reasoning, whether anything has actually been concluded, even when no one has labelled the fallacy for us and when it has been built by someone competent.

Houser, presenting Aristotle, treats logic as an art usable in any inquiry. It is also a science, the science of second intentions, which are found wherever anything is known. That is why this course will not begin from symbolic calculi. These have their use, but they are not the first form of an art that already lives in the language we speak. Formal logic, in Houser’s map, studies the structure of the three acts; material logic asks how certain the conclusion is, given the matter of the premises: demonstration when the conclusion is necessary, dialectic when it is probable, and sophistry when it only seems to be either.

Demonstration and persuasion must be kept distinct. A conclusion can follow necessarily from true premises and still fail to move a hearer, and a speech can move a hearer and still not have shown the thing. Much human discourse also involves persuasion, since even if we express the truth to someone, he may not be persuaded, and then we have not shown him the relevant truth.

Rhetoric will take up how a hearer is actually shown; logic keeps the first question from being confused with it. Anyone who wants to “win” before he can tell whether anything follows is asking rhetoric to do logic’s work. If that is already your difficulty, look ahead at the rhetoric pages, and then come back. If your terms have become muddy, so that you cannot tell what is being said of what, return to grammar and to Ars Grammatica. Logic cannot order what has not yet been said as a saying.

The order of word, concept, and thing that this art presupposes was set out in the foundation pages: words signify concepts immediately and things through them, and the logician works on words precisely insofar as they signify things through thoughts. Those pages also gave the first act its vocabulary (universal and singular, comprehension and extension of a concept taken as what is known, univocal and equivocal naming), which the practice application drills.
