Go and practice logic

The preceding account said what this art is for; the set of exercises is where it is practiced. Ars Syllogistica begins with the opening study, What Is Logic · An Orientation, the deck that the set of exercises itself marks “start here.” That deck is not scored. The next study is the First Act of the Mind · Simple Apprehension. Four exercises are open from the start: the Predicables, the Categories, Division, and Definition. They are to be worked through, and the syllogism is not to be passed over because it looks like “real logic.” The first two acts are why the third can be an act at all. Division and Definition lead into the Second Act (the proposition), and the Third Act (reasoning) waits until the second is passed.

If little grammar has been had, the set of exercises also offers a preliminary card, The Art of Grammar. This course has already gone through grammar, so that card is for use only if the terms will not remain distinct. Tracking on the Progress page may be enabled if the Guided Path is to remember the student; nothing is stored until that is done, and nothing leaves this browser.

The return to this course comes when one can say, in one's own words, what follows logically from what, and what only appears to. A good test is Conway’s accent on appears: if one can catch a paragraph that has the appearance of an argument and does not conclude, one has the question this lesson needed. The set of exercises then takes up opposition, immediate inference, and the syllogism, and this course will not duplicate those drills.

The place to begin is What Is Logic · An Orientation, which the set of exercises marks “start here,” then the First Act. The address is syllogistica.studiuminstitute.org.

Open Ars Syllogistica practice

The default recommendation is to finish a first pass of the First Act before going on to syllogisms. The return to grammar is for when names will not remain distinct. Rhetoric is to be looked at only when argument is being treated as a contest to be won rather than a discourse to be ordered, and even then the return follows. The cycle is the same as in grammar: doctrine, then practice, then doctrine again with better questions.

On returning

The student again decides when the first pass is done. The test is not the number of exercises passed but whether the following come to mind unprompted:

  • The three acts, in order, and what each produces: a definition, a statement, an argument.
  • Why a single term is neither true nor false, and what has to happen before anything can be.
  • The difference between a contradiction and a disagreement.
  • Conway’s accent on appears: what an expert fallacy looks like, as opposed to an amateur one.

If one can catch a paragraph that has the appearance of an argument and does not conclude, one that was not labeled in advance, one has what this lesson was for.

On the return

Two arts have now been practiced and not merely read. What has changed in the first is this: grammar looks different, because what logic needs from it has been seen. That is the ordinary shape of a liberal art (an art of the mind rather than of the hands), in which the earlier art is understood better from the later.

The next art is Rhetoric. The third art begins from a failure that the first two cannot repair: a thing well said and truly shown, and a hearer who has not seen it.

The return to logic is for whenever something has the appearance of an argument and one cannot say whether anything followed. And if, in the middle of an inference, one cannot tell what is being said of what, the trouble lies further back, in grammar, so that is where to go first.
