Go and practice logic

❧

The preceding account said what this art is for; the set of exercises is where we practice it. Ars Syllogistica begins with the opening study, What Is Logic · An Orientation, the deck that the set of exercises itself marks “start here.” That deck is not scored. The next study is the First Act of the Mind · Simple Apprehension. Four exercises are open from the start: the Predicables, the Categories, Division, and Definition. We work through them, and we do not pass over the syllogism because it looks like “real logic.” The first two acts are why the third can be an act at all. Division and Definition lead into the Second Act (the proposition), and the Third Act (reasoning) waits until the second is passed.

If we have had little grammar, the set of exercises also offers a preliminary card, The Art of Grammar. This course has already gone through grammar, so we use that card only if the terms will not remain distinct. We may enable tracking on the Progress page if we want the Guided Path to remember us; nothing is stored until we do that, and nothing leaves this browser.

We return to this course when we can say, in our own words, what follows logically from what, and what only appears to. A good test is Conway’s accent on appears: if we can catch a paragraph that has the appearance of an argument and does not conclude, we have the question this lesson needed. The set of exercises then takes up opposition, immediate inference, and the syllogism, and this course will not duplicate those drills.

The place to begin is What Is Logic · An Orientation, which the set of exercises marks “start here,” then the First Act. The address is syllogistica.studiuminstitute.org.

Open Ars Syllogistica practice

The default recommendation is to finish a first pass of the First Act before going on to syllogisms. We return to grammar when names will not remain distinct. We look at rhetoric only when we are treating argument as a contest to be won rather than a discourse to be ordered, and even then we return. The cycle is the same as in grammar: doctrine, then practice, then doctrine again with better questions.

When we come back

We again decide when the first pass is done. The test is not the number of exercises passed but whether the following come to mind unprompted:

  • The three acts, in order, and what each produces: a definition, a statement, an argument.
  • Why a single term is neither true nor false, and what has to happen before anything can be.
  • The difference between a contradiction and a disagreement.
  • Conway’s accent on appears: what an expert fallacy looks like, as opposed to an amateur one.

If we can catch a paragraph that has the appearance of an argument and does not conclude, one that was not labeled in advance, we have what this lesson was for.

When we come back

Two arts have now been practiced and not merely read. What has changed in the first is that grammar looks different, because we have seen what logic needs from it. That is the ordinary shape of a liberal art (an art of the mind rather than of the hands), in which the earlier art is understood better from the later.

The next art is Rhetoric. The third art begins from a failure that the first two cannot repair: a thing well said and truly shown, and a hearer who has not seen it.

We return to logic whenever something has the appearance of an argument and we cannot say whether anything followed. And if, in the middle of an inference, we cannot tell what is being said of what, the trouble lies further back, in grammar, so we go there first.
