What Puzzles and Studying Biases Cannot Teach

A puzzle, however, usually sets up the question for us in advance. The work of saying what a word means, whether a sentence is even a statement, and whether “all” and “some” and “only” and “if” have been used as they should be: all of these questions have already been answered by the setup of the puzzle.

The same is true of many “fallacy” lists. A name is given to an error in reasoning, and the student learns to put the name on a paragraph. But knowing a fallacy, how to avoid it, and how to correct it when we find it involves knowing how language works, how words get their meanings, and what sentences imply in their grammar. If we cannot say what the sentence was doing, the label of the fallacy is just a name for something we do not really understand.

Without proper attention to words and to the art of grammar, all our reasoning that has been trained on puzzles and logic games will rely on terms already set up, statements already taken for granted, and a purpose for the argument or statement already given. Then someone trained primarily in puzzles, logic games, or detecting biases discovers that he does not know what was being said, how things were being defined, and so on. He knows only a few strategies for helping himself find the truth, and he has sharpened his skills somewhat, but not on real arguments and reasoning. He may also confuse contradiction with disagreement and end up treating every pair of opposing views as if the two people had said opposite things about the same subject in the same respect.

Inference without the grammar of statements is an incomplete tool. This is part of why, even in modern logic books and courses, the grammar of a formal language is defined first, whether the language is being developed by logicians for rigorous thinking or as a programming language for precise instructions to computers.
