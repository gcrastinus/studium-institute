Why Names Are More than Labels

A label does not change the thing; it could be replaced and the thing would remain the same. But human naming does more than this. We teach a child a name so that the child can call for the thing, ask about it, distinguish it from what it is not, promise it, and be grateful for it. We argue about names because a bad name will direct the mind to the wrong object or to no object at all.

Consider what happens when someone says “never mind the words, just look at the thing.” He is still using words to point, since he has no choice. Some things can be picked up and given to another person, but most things cannot; and most of what we speak about, namely the various aspects of things, cannot be picked up at all. For example, we can point to the color of an apple, but that is all we can do with that color as such. We can speak, write, draw, and number, and drawing and numbering are already kinds of saying. The instruction to skip the name is itself a name for an act, the act of ignoring words; and it works only if the hearer already shares enough speech to know where to look. If we ignored words completely, we would be unable to speak or to hear.

If words were only labels, so that I pass one to you and all you need to do to receive it is to hear the sound, teaching would be impossible. A lesson is not the transfer of a physical object from one mind to another. Teaching is speech in which two people come to mean the same thing by the same words, well enough to go on; that is, the student has to understand what the teacher means, not just take in the sounds of the words. When that fails, we do not say that the two have different labels for the same thing; we say that the student has not yet understood the teacher.

Our concern here is only to set the direction of our inquiry, and so we do not yet consider in detail the relation between names and things. A name is a sign, as we will see later.

The account of words, concepts, and things is in the foundation, at Words, Concepts, and Things and Words as Signs. It is drawn from Aristotle and Aquinas, with Walshe, Andres, Houser, and Maritain as guides.
