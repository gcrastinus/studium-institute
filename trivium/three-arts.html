How they fit

The Three Arts Together

Grammar, logic, and rhetoric each answer a different question about the same arrangement of words. Grammar asks whether something is correctly said. Logic asks whether what is said is true and whether its conclusions follow from its premises. Rhetoric asks whether a hearer has been persuaded of it. Together they are the foundations of how we express ourselves, how we show that something is true, and how we persuade others of that truth.

Learning the arts

Each art is learned in two ways: as an account and as the art to be practiced. Often students move from one art to the other and later return to the earlier art with better questions and better insights about the whole of discourse and how its parts relate to each other.

  • Grammar: Grammar, then Ars Grammatica, beginning with What Grammar Is.
  • Logic: Logic, then Ars Logica, beginning with What Is Logic · An Orientation.
  • Rhetoric: Rhetoric, then Ars Rhetorica, beginning with Act I, What Rhetoric Is.

The order of the arts

The classical order is grammar, then logic, then rhetoric, because a statement is made of words and because a hearer should be persuaded only of what has been shown. It is not the only defensible order. Houser, in his own modern textbook, puts rhetoric before logic, because students now meet so much speech designed to move them that they need to recognize ethos, pathos, and logos early. That is a reasonable choice, provided we then go on to logic. This course keeps the classical order, because rhetoric ordered to the truth needs a truth to be ordered toward, and logic is the art that establishes it.
