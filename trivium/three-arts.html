How they fit

Grammar, logic, and rhetoric

❧

We now have three questions that look alike and are not: Is this joined as speech? Does this follow logically, and is it so? Has a hearer been shown it? They are the foundations of how we come to express ourselves, to show that something is true, and to persuade others of that truth. Their school names are grammar, logic, and rhetoric. They are not three subjects that merely happen to be studied together, but one education in speech, taken at three depths of the same act.

ObjectActFruit
GrammarSignificant speech and its compositionWords are arranged so that something is saidA well-formed utterance (congruity)
LogicDiscourse in its three acts: term, statement, argumentOrdering discourse toward truthDefinition, true statement, and a conclusion that follows
RhetoricThis case, these hearersFinding and ordering the available means of persuasionAssent ordered to the true and the good (the hearer moved to see it)

The sets of exercises put the same distinction in the form of a test. Grammar is satisfied by congruity and fails as solecism (a fault in how words are joined) or barbarism (a fault in a single word). Logic is satisfied by truth and valid following (the conclusion following necessarily from the premises), and fails as falsity or fallacy. Rhetoric is satisfied by persuasion ordered to what is so, and fails as ineffectiveness or, if it persuades toward what is false, as sophistry. Walshe’s pair still holds: “man is stone” can be well formed and false; “man are animal” can be ill formed and true. Aristotle’s definition still holds as well: rhetoric sees the available means in this case; it does not replace demonstration, and it is not the art of winning.

The cycle

Each art is learned twice: once as doctrine in these accounts, and once as practice in the matching set of exercises. Then we return. The return is not a failure of the first pass; it is how an art is acquired. A new confusion in the set of exercises sends us back to the doctrine, and a new distinction in the doctrine sends us back to the set of exercises with a better question.

For grammar, the reading is What grammar is here, and then the practice is Ars Grammatica, beginning with What Grammar Is. We return when we can say what grammar asks that the other two arts do not.

For logic, the reading is Logic as a liberal art, and then the practice is Ars Syllogistica, beginning with What Is Logic · An Orientation and the First Act. We return when we can tell what follows from what, and what only appears to.

For rhetoric, the reading is Rhetoric ordered to the true and the good, and then the practice is Ars Rhetorica, beginning at Act I, What Rhetoric Is, with the tutorial card The counterpart of dialectic. We return when we can say what still has to be done for these hearers after the saying is joined and the inference is valid.

We should not finish the set of exercises before we know the question, and the doctrine is not a substitute for the drills. The default order remains grammar, then logic, then rhetoric, because a statement is made of words, and a hearer is shown a claim that can be followed.

If something is difficult

If we cannot tell what function a word has in a sentence, or whether a string is even a complete saying, we are not yet in logic or rhetoric. We return to grammar and to Ars Grammatica.

If we cannot yet tell a statement from a question or a command, we remain with grammar until we can make the distinction, and then we may take up the opening of logic, since logic works on statements, which can be true or false. That case is rare, and it is not a way of skipping the first art.

If we can form sentences but cannot tell contradiction from disagreement, or what follows from what, or if a paragraph has the appearance of an argument and none of its necessity, we return to logic and to Ars Syllogistica.

If the terms have become unclear in the middle of an inference, so that we cannot tell what is being said of what, we leave the syllogism and return to grammar. Logic cannot order what has not yet been said as a saying.

If we can follow an inference but cannot offer it to anyone, or if we address others without having shown anything, we turn to rhetoric and to Ars Rhetorica.

If we want to win before we can follow an inference, we are turning to rhetoric too early. We remain with logic until something follows; then rhetoric will have a claim to show. If empty debate (fluency without meaning) is already the difficulty, a first pass of rhetoric shows what the art is for, and then we return to grammar and logic, so that we have something true to persuade toward.

On the order itself

The order grammar, logic, rhetoric is the one this course recommends and has argued for: a statement is made of words, and a hearer is shown a claim that can be followed. It is also the traditional order. But it is not the only defensible one, and the reader should know that before treating it as settled.

R. E. Houser, whose account of the three acts this course has used throughout, orders his own textbook grammar, then rhetoric, then logic, and his reason is not antiquarian. He tells instructors that the “information revolution” makes it more necessary than ever to distinguish ethos, pathos, and logos, and he asks them to cut later material rather than cut the rhetorical appeals. His point is that a student now meets, every day and in volume, speech engineered to move him, and that he needs to be able to name what is being done to him before he has finished a course in the syllogism.

That argument has force, and it has most force for the reader who has come here from a life spent in argument online. For such a reader, taking a first pass of rhetoric after grammar and before logic is a reasonable choice, not a shortcut, provided he comes back. The reason this course still recommends logic second is narrower than Houser’s reason is broad: rhetoric ordered to the true and the good needs something true to be ordered toward, and the art of telling what follows is what supplies it. We should take an order that we can defend, and we should be able to state the reason.

If we want methods, data, or systems before we can name, we are turning to the later sciences too early. The quadrivium is postponed on purpose; words remain the study until speech is capable of truth.

The test is an ordinary one. After a stretch of practice, we ask which of the three questions we cannot yet answer; that is the art to return to. Then we take up the matching set of exercises, and then we return again.
